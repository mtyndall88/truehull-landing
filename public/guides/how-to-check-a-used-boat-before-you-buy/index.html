How to check a used boat before you buy

By TrueHull Updated August 24, 2026 About a 9 minute read

Buying a used boat means trusting a stranger's account of something most people cannot easily inspect. Cars generally have standardized VIN-based records and state titles. Boat records are less uniform: titling and registration requirements vary by state, ownership can cross jurisdictions, and there is no single history system that follows every boat. Add the pressure to move fast on a good listing, and it is easy to skip steps. The good news is that a careful buyer's process is short and repeatable. Work through it before any money moves, and a stressful gamble becomes a calm checklist.

Here is the order that usually matters most: match the paperwork, pull the official records, check the boat's storm exposure, read and match the hull identification number, and then inspect and sea trial before you commit. None of this replaces a professional marine survey. It helps you arrive at one better prepared, and it helps you walk away early when the answers do not add up.

1. Match the paperwork before you look at the boat

Before you drive to see a single hull, ask the seller for photos of the documents that describe the boat on paper: the state title if the vessel is state-titled, the current registration, a clear shot of the hull identification number, and, if the vessel is federally documented, its current Certificate of Documentation and official number. Then check that the names and numbers agree with each other and with the hull itself. Small mismatches are where trouble hides.

  • The seller's name should match the name on the title and registration. If it does not, ask why before you go further.
  • The hull identification number should be identical everywhere it appears, letter for letter.
  • Ask directly whether there is a loan or lien against the boat, the trailer, or the engine, and get the answer in writing.

2. Pull the official records, and know their limits

Boat records do not live in one place, which is exactly why buyers get stuck here. Different facts sit with different authorities, and each uses its own identifier and its own process.

  • Title and registration records are generally handled at the state level, though how you access them, and whether an online lookup exists, varies by state. Lien and security-interest records depend on the vessel's documentation status and applicable law: for federally documented vessels, the Coast Guard's National Vessel Documentation Center maintains recorded mortgages and other filed instruments.
  • Theft records are primarily maintained through law-enforcement systems. The FBI's NCIC Boat File contains records of stolen boats, but NCIC is not a public consumer database. The NICB's free VINCheck service may contain records for some watercraft reported as stolen or salvage by participating insurers, but it is not a comprehensive boat-history search.
  • Federal documentation is a separate national registration system administered by the Coast Guard. Vessels of at least five net tons engaged in certain coastwise trade or fisheries generally must be documented unless exempt, while eligible vessels of at least five net tons may also be federally documented for recreational use. For a documented boat, the Coast Guard's National Vessel Documentation Center holds the record and can issue an abstract of title showing the vessel's ownership and encumbrance history, for a modest fee. Use that official Coast Guard site, not the paid lookalike services that rank around it in search results.

3. Check the boat's storm exposure

A boat that sat through a major hurricane can carry damage a quick look never shows, and storm damage often leaves no paper trail at all, especially when it was never insured or claimed. This is where boats differ from cars: geography and timing matter. Ask where the boat was kept, and whether it was hauled out, trailered, or left in the water during past storm seasons. If a location and a date line up with a named storm that passed nearby, ask directly about water intrusion, repairs, and any insurance claims since.

Storm history is public, but it is scattered across seasons and datasets and easy to overlook. Knowing which named storms passed near a boat's home waters, and when, turns a vague worry into a specific question you can put to the seller and to a surveyor. It is worth saying plainly: a clean title does not rule out storm damage, and exposure to a storm is not the same as proof of damage. The point is to know where to look, not to assume the worst.

4. Read and match the hull identification number

Every U.S. boat built or imported since November 1972 carries a 12-character hull identification number, usually stamped on the upper starboard corner of the transom. The HIN is not a decoder ring for the boat's past, but it is the key that ties the boat to most official records, and its format can tell you whether the number conforms to the expected HIN structure. Confirm that the characters on the transom match the characters on the title and registration, exactly. A number that does not match, that looks ground down or re-stamped, or that is missing on a boat new enough to require one, is a reason to slow down and ask hard questions.

5. Inspect, then sea trial, with a professional

Paperwork and records tell you what to worry about. The boat tells you the rest, and some of what matters most is only visible on the water. Walk the hull for soft spots, look hard at the transom and stringers, check for signs of past submersion, and compare the engine hours against the wear you can see. For any purchase that matters, hire an accredited marine surveyor and insist on a sea trial. A survey is not free, but it is a small fraction of the boat's price, and it can uncover problems that cost far more than the survey itself. Many lenders and insurers require a marine survey for used boats, depending on factors such as the boat's age, size, value, and the lender or insurer's underwriting requirements.

You can learn what a survey covers from Discover Boating's overview of marine surveys, and find a qualified marine surveyor through professional bodies such as SAMS (the Society of Accredited Marine Surveyors) or NAMS. Brokers, lenders, and insurers can also point you to surveyors they trust.

6. Ask the questions records cannot answer

The right questions surface the things no database holds. Keep them plain and specific, and pay as much attention to how they are answered as to the answers themselves.

  • Why are you selling, and how long have you owned it?
  • Where has it been kept, and was it ever through a major storm?
  • Have there been any insurance claims, repairs, or water intrusion you know of?
  • Is there a lien on the boat, the trailer, or the engine?
  • Can I see maintenance records and the dates of the last service?

What no report, including TrueHull, can tell you

It is worth being honest about the limits of any tool in this space, including ours. There is no national boat-history database that catches everything, records are incomplete by nature, and the absence of a record is not evidence that nothing happened. That is why this whole process ends with a person: a surveyor on the boat, a mechanic on the engine, and your own read of the seller. Frequently asked questions

Can you check a used boat's history by its HIN for free?

The hull identification number is the key to most records, but there is no single free national boat-history lookup. You use the HIN to pull state title and registration records, to check federal documentation when the vessel is federally documented, and to confirm the number matches the paperwork and the transom. Paid report services exist, none is complete, and a missing record never proves a clean history.

Do I really need a marine survey on an inexpensive boat?

A survey is a judgment call on lower-priced boats, but cheap boats are often the ones hiding the most expensive problems. At a minimum, have an experienced boater or mechanic look it over and insist on a sea trial. Many lenders and insurers require a marine survey for used boats, depending on factors such as the boat's age, size, value, and the lender or insurer's underwriting requirements.

How can I tell if a used boat was in a hurricane?

Records rarely say so directly. Ask where the boat was kept and whether it was hauled out during past storm seasons, compare those locations and dates against named storms that passed nearby, and have a surveyor check for water intrusion and repairs. A clean title does not rule out storm damage.

Does a clean title mean a used boat is problem-free?

No. Boat titling varies by state, some states do not title boats at all, and damage often never reaches a title. A clean title is a good sign, not a guarantee, which is why the physical inspection and the seller conversation still matter.

What is a HIN and where do I find it?

The hull identification number is a 12-character code required on U.S. boats built or imported since November 1972. It is usually on the upper starboard corner of the transom, and it should match the number on the title and registration exactly.
